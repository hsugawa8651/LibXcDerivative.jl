# Derivatives

Taking derivatives is what this package is for. Libxc generates separate C
for each derivative order, and stops: the build `Libxc.jl` vendors returns
`vrho` and `v2rho2` and nothing above. In this package there is one
expression per functional, and every derivative is that expression
differentiated.

That difference is in *when* the differentiating happens, not in whether it is
symbolic. Libxc's derivative code is generated by differentiating a symbolic
form once, when Libxc is built, with Maple since version 4 and with SymPy in
the pinned 7.1.2 sources, and which orders exist is a compile-time choice:
`MAXORDER`, default 2 and at most 4 (`CMakeLists.txt:75`). In this package
the expression is differentiated at the call, by the caller's own tool, so the
order is a property of the call rather than of the build.

`Libxc.jl` inherits both ends of that. It carries no differentiation rules of
its own, and its `evaluate!` methods are typed `rho::Array{Float64}`, so a dual
number cannot enter them: `ForwardDiff.derivative` over `Libxc.evaluate` is a
`MethodError`, not a wrong number. Writing rules for it is possible in
principle, since the library returns `vrho` itself, and nothing ships them.

## The scalar entry points differentiate with anything

A functional is plain arithmetic over `Float64`, so it goes through whatever
automatic differentiation the caller already has, with no extension and no
cooperation from this package:

```jldoctest
julia> using ForwardDiff

julia> ForwardDiff.derivative(r -> r * lda_x(r), 0.5)
-0.7815926417967739
```

That is Libxc's `vrho`, and the `r *` in front of it is the whole subtlety:

!!! warning "Libxc's convention, which is easy to get wrong"
    `zk` is the energy **per particle**. Libxc's `vrho` is the derivative of
    `rho * zk`, not of `zk`. `vsigma`, `vlapl` and `vtau` are `rho` times the
    derivative of `zk`. Spin-polarized, `vrho[s]` is the derivative of
    `rho_total * zk` with respect to channel `s`.

Getting that wrong gives a plausible potential that is wrong by a term, which
is why the named-slot layer below exists: it states the convention once.

## What your AD can do

| your AD | what works | extension needed |
|---|---|---|
| none | values, `<name>_defaults`, the fact layer, mixing coefficients, [`libxc_clamp`](@ref) | — |
| **ForwardDiff** | everything, including the whole named-slot layer | `ForwardDiff` |
| **Zygote** | the scalar entry points; **not** the slot layer | **`ChainRulesCore`** |
| **Symbolics** | symbolic tracing through a functional | `Symbolics` |
| Enzyme and others | untested | — |

Two asymmetries in that table are worth stating plainly, because neither is
guessable:

* **Zygote needs `ChainRulesCore` loaded**, not for convenience but to work at
  all: the rule for `NaNMath.sqrt` lives there, and without it every kernel
  containing a square root fails. ForwardDiff is unaffected, having its own
  `DiffRules` entry. Of the 154 functionals Libxc evaluates as a weighted sum,
  the ones with a `<name>_components`, 134 differentiate under Zygote and 20
  raise an `OverflowError` in `Rational{Int128}`, from differentiating the `//`
  literals the kernels carry rather than from anything about the density. They
  are the `mpw` and `pw` families, `gga_xc_xlyp` and `gga_xc_ncap` among
  them.
* **The slot layer is ForwardDiff-only.** It differentiates internally rather
  than being differentiated, so the choice is the package's, not the caller's.
  A Zygote user who wants `xc_fxc` still writes `using ForwardDiff`.

### Without the extension the names exist but have no methods

`xc_exc_vxc`, `xc_fxc`, `xc_kxc`, `xc_lxc`, `slot_names` and `slot_widths` are
declared in the package and implemented in the ForwardDiff extension. Load the
package alone and each has **zero methods**, so calling one raises a
`MethodError` rather than anything that mentions an extension. `using
ForwardDiff` is what makes them exist.

`dft_functional` is the same arrangement under a different extension: it has
zero methods until `DftFunctionals` *and* `ComponentArrays` are both loaded, and
ForwardDiff does nothing for it.

[`libxc_clamp`](@ref) is the exception that needs no extension at all: it is
arithmetic, and it has its three methods in the package itself.

## With respect to the parameters, which Libxc cannot be asked for

A functional's parameters are ordinary arguments here, so they differentiate
the way the density does. In Libxc they are constants by the time the
derivative code is emitted, so there is nothing to ask it for.

```jldoctest
julia> using ForwardDiff

julia> p = gga_x_pbe_defaults;

julia> ForwardDiff.derivative(k -> gga_x_pbe(0.5, 0.1; params = merge(p, (; params_a_kappa = k))), p.params_a_kappa)
-1.1912752580356732e-5

julia> ForwardDiff.derivative(m -> gga_x_pbe(0.5, 0.1; params = merge(p, (; params_a_mu = m))), p.params_a_mu)
-0.00963510812734225
```

How far that reaches, measured over the whole corpus at one unpolarized point
(`rho = 0.5`, `sigma = 0.05`, `lapl = 0.025`, `tau = 0.15`), one
`ForwardDiff.gradient` over every parameter each functional carries:

* **425 of the 697 identifiers carry parameters of their own**, 3507 of them
  between the lot, and the gradient came back for every one: nothing raised,
  nothing infinite, no `NaN`.
* **154 carry none because they are the mixes.** Their entry points take no
  `params` at all: Libxc keeps the weights outside every kernel, so they are
  literals in the generated sum. The components are entry points in their own
  right, and that is where their parameters are.
* **the other 118 have no parameter to differentiate.** Their numbers sit in
  the expression, exactly as they sit in Libxc's C: `lda_c_vwn`, `gga_x_g96`,
  `mgga_c_scan`.
* **317 of the 3507 components come back exactly zero**, which is the point
  and not the parameter. A polarized row cannot enter at zero polarization
  (`params_a_ferro_*`, and the `_1` rows of the PW, PZ and KSDT families), and
  an exponent of `rs` enters through a `log(rs)` that vanishes where `rs == 1`,
  which `rho = 0.5` is in one dimension. At a polarized point with another
  density, 3409 components are nonzero and 86 are zero.

The seven potential-only entry points have an unpolarized method only, so
their parameters differentiate in that form.

### All of them at once, by name

`param_gradient` is that derivative for every parameter a functional carries,
without the caller writing any name out. `param_names` says which they are and
in what order, `param_point` is where a fit starts from, and `params_from`
turns a vector back into the `params` an entry point takes.

```jldoctest
julia> param_gradient(gga_x_pbe, 0.5, 0.1)
(params_a_kappa = -1.1912752580356732e-5, params_a_mu = -0.00963510812734225)

julia> param_names(gga_x_pbe)
(:params_a_kappa, :params_a_mu)

julia> length(param_names(lda_xc_ksdt))
38

julia> param_point(gga_x_pbe) == [0.804, 0.2195149727645171]
true

julia> params_from(gga_x_pbe, [0.9, 0.2]) === merge(gga_x_pbe_defaults, (params_a_kappa = 0.9, params_a_mu = 0.2))
true
```

`params` moves the point the derivative is taken at, not which parameters are
differentiated: that is always all of them. A functional with none of its own
is refused rather than answered with an empty tuple, and a parameter with no
default has to be given a value, since there is no point to differentiate at
otherwise.

Fitting is what these are for, and there the shape of the call matters.
`params` is a NamedTuple, and a NamedTuple's names are part of its type:
writing them out, or letting `params_from` write them, costs nothing over
passing no parameters at all. Building the same tuple from names the compiler
cannot see (`NamedTuple{own}(Tuple(q))` for an `own` that is an ordinary
value) costs several times the call itself, and the gradient over it several
times the gradient. `param_gradient` builds its point on every call, which a
loop need not pay for:

```julia
q = param_point(gga_x_pbe)
ForwardDiff.gradient(v -> gga_x_pbe(0.5, 0.1; params = params_from(gga_x_pbe, v)), q)
```

## The named slots

```jldoctest
julia> using ForwardDiff

julia> xc_exc_vxc(gga_x_pbe, 1.0, 0.2)
(zk = -0.739404537509313, vrho = -0.983620539433825, vsigma = -0.0042228323348605734)

julia> xc_fxc(lda_x, 0.5)
(v2rho2 = -0.5210617611978494,)

julia> xc_kxc(lda_x, 0.5)
(v3rho3 = 0.694749014930464,)
```

A spin-polarized density arrives as one tuple per quantity and comes back the
same way:

```jldoctest
julia> using ForwardDiff

julia> xc_exc_vxc(lda_x, (0.3, 0.2))
(zk = -0.5914246540028067, vrho = (-0.8305661184154168, -0.7255663357195637))
```

The field set is fixed by the family and the order, and can be had without
evaluating anything:

```jldoctest
julia> using ForwardDiff

julia> slot_names(:gga, 2)
(:v2rho2, :v2rhosigma, :v2sigma2)

julia> length(slot_names(:mgga, 4)), sum(slot_widths(:mgga, 4))
(35, 495)
```

## What it costs, and why

The cost is **compiling derivative chains**, one specialization per component,
paid on the first call for a given functional, order and spin case, and only
if that call is made. It is not the arithmetic: the second call is four orders
of magnitude cheaper.

Measured just now on a polarized meta-GGA fourth derivative (`mgga_c_cs`, one
8-core machine under moderate load):

| call | components | first call | again |
|---|---|---|---|
| `xc_lxc(f, :v4rho4, …)` | 5 | **2.4 s** | 67 µs |
| `xc_lxc(f, …)`, all 35 slots | 495 | **50.8 s** | 36 ms |

So there is a form that asks for one slot by name:

```julia
xc_lxc(mgga_c_cs, :v4rho4, (na, nb), (gaa, gab, gbb), (la, lb), (ta, tb))
```

Twenty times cheaper here, for the one slot in twenty that was wanted. The
whole tuple is assembled from these same methods, so the two cannot disagree
about a number. Neither `libxc` nor `Libxc.jl` has this entry point: both
always fill every slot.

!!! warning "Third and fourth derivatives of `hyb_gga_xc_hse06` cost minutes"
    What the first call spends is compile time, and this one functional holds
    the 158 KiB `gga_x_wpbeh` kernel: its third derivative took 260 s and its
    fourth was stopped after twelve minutes. The rest compiles in seconds, a
    polarized third derivative of 165 components in 18.7 s.

### And what the arithmetic costs, against the C library

Compiling is paid once; arithmetic is paid per point, and one expression
differentiated at the call is slower than C generated per order. Measured
against a build of the pinned sources (7.1.2), both sides in one process. The
figures below are machine-dependent, as are the ratios: read them for the two
shapes named under the table rather than as numbers to reproduce.

| order | C, ns/point | this package, ns/point | **ratio** |
|---|---|---|---|
| `exc`, six functionals | 12.3–297.3 | 26.7–502.5 | **1.7–4.6** |
| `vxc`, four | 12.4–73.6 | 72.0–649.3 | **5.8–8.8** |
| `fxc`, four | 14.3–102.2 | 53.5–1013.6 | **3.7–22** |
| `kxc`, four | 19.6–201.1 | 128.4–3862.9 | **6.6–77** |

The functionals are `lda_x`, `lda_c_pw`, `gga_x_pbe`, `gga_c_pbe` at every
order, plus `mgga_x_scan` and `mgga_c_tpss` at the first. Over `rho`
log-spaced on `[1e-3, 10]` with `sigma = rho^(8/3)` (so the reduced gradient is
1), `tau = 6 sigma / rho` and `lapl = rho^(5/3)`; N = 100,000 points for the
first two orders and 20,000 for the others, the minimum of five runs. The C
side gets one vectorised call over all N points, which is its interface, while
the entry point here is called once per point: the asymmetry is how each is
meant to be used. The `kxc` row is against a `MAXORDER=3` build, since the
default one has no third derivative to compare with at all.

Two shapes in those numbers. **The heavier the expression, the narrower the
gap**: `mgga_c_tpss`, the heaviest energy here, is 1.69, where `lda_c_pw` is
4.57, since the fixed cost of the reduced variables and of `NaNMath.pow` is
a smaller share of a long expression. And **the gap is widest where C is
cheapest**: `gga_x_pbe`'s third derivative costs C 37.5 ns and costs this
package 2871.6, while `gga_c_pbe`, four times dearer in C, is 19 rather than 77
times slower here.

Over that same sweep both sides agree to 3e-15 at the first two orders, except
the two components differentiated twice in `sigma`, which reach 1.7e-14.
[How closely it matches Libxc](@ref) says how those figures are taken, and what
happens to them at third order.

## Libxc clamps its input, and so does this

For a functional that reads `tau`, Libxc does not hand the caller's numbers to
the kernel unchanged: it clamps the gradient to what the kinetic energy density
allows, `sigma ← min(sigma, 8·rho·tau)`.

```jldoctest
julia> libxc_clamp(mgga_x_tpss, 0.5, 1.0, 0.0, 0.05)   # 8*rho*tau = 0.2
(0.5, 0.2, 0.0, 0.05)

julia> libxc_clamp(gga_x_pbe, 0.5, 1.0, 0.0, 0.05)     # no tau, nothing to clamp
(0.5, 1.0, 0.0, 0.05)
```

Any real density satisfies the von Weizsäcker bound `tau ≥ sigma/(8·rho)`, so
this does nothing to physical input. Above the bound it is the difference
between answering as Libxc does and carrying an unphysical gradient through the
expression: 15 % on `hyb_mgga_x_bmk` at five times the bound, a factor of
eleven on `hyb_mgga_x_cf22d`. The slot functions apply it themselves and take
`enforce_fhc=false` to stop them.

Whether Libxc enforces this at all is a **setting of its build**, not a
property of the functional; [What is Libxc's and what is ours](@ref) says why
that distinction keeps mattering.
